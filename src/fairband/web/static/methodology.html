One engine values every company, with one route per phase (a route is the method for one stage of a company's life). The inputs change with the phase, because a young company and an old one show different facts. The arithmetic does not change. Every route sums the cash a company can give its owners, year by year. It discounts that cash at the hurdle rate (the yearly return you require), —.

A value here is what the business can earn, less what its growth costs, discounted at the hurdle rate. It is not a prediction of the price. A price can stay far from the value for years. The screen says where the price stands against the value; what to do about it is your decision.

The life cycle

Aswath Damodaran describes a company's life in six phases [Damodaran 2024]. Two figures place a company: how fast its revenue grows, and whether it makes an operating profit. In each phase, the market prices a different thing. Under each phase is its count of companies in the latest run.

  1. Start-up

    An idea is becoming a business. Revenue is small or nil, and the company spends to build its product. What matters most: the size of the potential market and access to capital.

    Companies in the latest run: —

  2. Young Growth

    The product has found buyers, and revenue grows fast. Losses continue, because building the business costs more than it brings in. What matters most: revenue growth and gross margins.

    Companies in the latest run: —

  3. High Growth

    Revenue still grows fast, and the business earns its first operating profit. Margins widen as it grows. Fairband puts a company here while its operating margin is still below zero but rising. Once the margin is positive, the company is Mature Growth or Mature Stable. What matters most: revenue growth and operating margins.

    Companies in the latest run: —

  4. Mature Growth

    Growth slows but stays above the economy's. Profits are large, and earnings grow with revenue. What matters most: earnings growth.

    Companies in the latest run: —

  5. Mature Stable

    Revenue grows with the economy, or a little more slowly. Profits are steady, and cash goes back to the owners. What matters most: the stability of earnings.

    Companies in the latest run: —

  6. Decline

    Revenue shrinks year after year, and margins shrink with it. The company sells assets, returns cash, or both. What matters most: book value (what the company owns, as its balance sheet records it).

    Companies in the latest run: —

— companies have no phase in the latest run. Their figures cannot support one, as the next section explains.

How a company gets its phase

The classifier (the part of the engine that assigns the phase) reads the fiscal-year statements. It looks at revenue growth first, then at the operating margin. These are Damodaran's two deciding figures [Damodaran 2024].

Revenue growth and the growth word

Revenue growth is the compound yearly rate over the last three fiscal years. At each full refresh, Fairband ranks every company's growth against the universe (all the companies the screen covers). The ranking cuts the companies into ten groups of equal size, called deciles. Each pair of deciles has a growth word.

The margin and its direction

The operating margin is operating income divided by revenue. The margin that decides is the newest one among the last five fiscal years. Its trend is the slope of the yearly margins over those five years. The slopes of the companies with a positive margin are ranked into deciles too. A rising slope in the top two deciles reads Rising. A falling slope in the bottom two reads Declining. Any other slope reads Stable. A stable margin points to no phase.

The rules

The first rule that fits decides the phase.

  1. A company with no revenue now, or none three years ago, is a Start-up. An established company (rule 4 says which) is never one. With no revenue three years ago, it moves to rule 2 with a note. Its growth runs over its years of revenue since then. With no revenue now, it stays unclassified.
  2. A company is in Decline when three things hold. Its growth word is Close to zero or negative. Its five-year revenue trend falls. Its revenue fell in at least three of its last four years.
  3. A company with no operating margin in its last five fiscal years stays unclassified.
  4. A company with a negative margin is High Growth when its margin trend rises, else Young Growth. The exception is an established company. Established means a positive margin in at least three of the last five years, or in at least five of the fiscal years on file. Such a company moves to rule 5 as if its margin were positive, with a note that names the loss year.
  5. A company with a margin of zero or more is Mature Growth when its growth word is Very High, High or Moderate. It is Mature Stable when the word is Low or Close to zero or negative.

Notes that do not change the phase

Some checks add a note and never change the phase. The notes show in the drawer's phase evidence.

  • The growth word points to another phase than the margin rule gave a loss-making company.
  • The margin direction points elsewhere: Rising to Mature Growth, Declining to Decline.
  • Free cash flow has the unusual sign for the phase, such as positive in Young Growth.
  • Revenue fell over five years, but in too few years to make a Decline.
  • An established company had a loss year and was placed as if it were profitable.
  • An established company had no revenue three years ago and was not made a Start-up.
  • The history is short, the margin is exactly zero, or the reporting currency changed.

Beside the phase, the drawer shows the Dickinson stage (a life-cycle reading of the signs of three cash flows) [Dickinson 2011]. It reads the last fiscal year's operating, investing and financing cash flows. It never decides the phase and adds no note.

When a company stays unclassified

A company stays unclassified when its figures cannot support a phase. That happens with fewer than two fiscal years, no positive revenue in the last year, no measurable growth, or no operating margin. An unclassified company gets no value; its row gives the reason instead.

What a value means here

The hurdle rate and terminal growth

Every route discounts at the hurdle rate, —. Terminal growth (the rate a company is assumed to grow at forever, after the forecast years) is —. Terminal growth must stay below the hurdle rate, or the value would have no limit.

The reinvestment charge

Growth costs money: a company must invest to grow. The reinvestment charge (the share of earnings held back to pay for growth) is next year's revenue growth over the return on new capital [McKinsey]. New sales need new capital [Damodaran templates]. Earnings that grow faster than revenue come from wider margins on the capital already in place, which costs nothing. What is left is cash the owners can take. Here is the arithmetic for one year.

  1. Earnings this year are 100.
  2. Next year's revenue growth is 6.0%, and new capital earns 15.0%.
  3. The charge is 6.0% ÷ 15.0% = 40.0%, so the company reinvests 40 and keeps 60.
  4. The route discounts the 60 kept, not the 100 earned, at the hurdle rate of —.

When new capital earns only the hurdle rate, growth adds nothing to the value. When it earns more, growth adds value. A company with a lasting edge earns more for longer.

When growth outruns the return, the charge is more than the earnings. That year pays out less than nothing: the owners put money in to fund the growth.

The return on new capital holds while growth stays above the belief. Then it fades in equal steps to the terminal return by the end of the runway [Damodaran templates]. A company with no growth above the belief starts the fade in year 1.

The moat and the runway

A moat is a lasting edge over competitors [Mauboussin and Callahan 2016]. Fairband reads it from the GuruFocus Moat Score. A score of — or more is Wide, — or more is Narrow, and any lower score is None. The moat sets the runway (the years a company keeps growing faster than terminal growth). The runway is — with no moat, — with a Narrow moat and — with a Wide moat. The runway sets the forecast's length on the Mature Growth and Mature Stable routes.

After the runway, new capital earns the hurdle rate. A company with a Wide moat may earn up to — more, so at most —.

The levels

The margin of safety (the discount to the value you ask before you buy) also follows the moat. It is — with no moat, — with a Narrow moat and — with a Wide moat. The buy level is the value less the margin of safety. The fair band runs from — to — the value. The trim level is — the value.

The designation (the word for where the price stands against the value) reads the price divided by the value.

The validity window

A value holds until the company's next earnings date. Without a known date, it holds for — after the statements were last read. It reads current until — of that window has passed, then aging. It reads expired after the window ends, and keeps its figures until the next refresh.

Refusals

A refusal (a row with no value and a stated reason) is an answer, not an error. The row stays on the screen with its price and the figures the engine reached. These are the reasons, in words.

  • No ranking yet: no full refresh has ranked the universe.
  • No phase: the company's figures cannot support one.
  • A foreign filer: the company reports in another currency, or its shares trade as depositary receipts.
  • Missing figures: no price, no share count, no trailing revenue or earnings, or no tax rate for the country.
  • Incomplete forecasts: the analysts' forecasts miss part of the next twelve months, or turn negative.
  • Too few peers: not enough mature companies to compare the company with.
  • No positive value: the route's value comes to zero or less.
  • Start-up: no value by design, as the Start-up section explains.
  • A failed fetch: the data vendor did not deliver a required statement or the price.

Mature Growth

In Mature Growth, the market prices earnings growth [Damodaran 2024]. The route forecasts net income year by year. Each year pays for the growth of the year after.

The steps

  1. Year 1 is the analysts' consensus (their average forecast) of earnings for the next twelve months.
  2. Years 2 and 3 grow at the consensus growth for the next two fiscal years.
  3. From year 4, growth fades in equal steps over — to the belief (the long-run growth the engine assumes).
  4. The belief is the median ten-year earnings growth of companies with similar sales [Base Rate Book]. It adds — for inflation.
  5. The belief holds to the end of the runway.
  6. Each year pays the reinvestment charge for next year's revenue growth. Years 2 and 3 take the consensus revenue growth; later years fade to the belief.
  7. The charge starts at the runway return (the company's own return on new capital, at least the hurdle rate). It holds while growth is above the belief, then fades to the terminal return.
  8. After the runway, earnings grow at terminal growth, charged at the terminal return (the hurdle rate, or more for a Wide moat).
  9. Each year's cash and the terminal value are discounted at the hurdle rate and summed. The sum divided by the diluted share count is the value a share.

The runway return is the return on the capital added over five years. When that measure is unreliable, it is the average return of the last three years.

What the price needs

The implied growth is the belief at which the value equals today's price. Set it beside the belief. If the price needs far more growth than similar companies usually reach, it assumes the company is an exception. If it needs less, the value covers the price even when growth falls below the belief, down to the implied growth.

The drawer also shows the value at runways of —, — and —. It shows the value at returns of — and — too. A wide spread means the value depends heavily on those choices.

Flags on this route

A consensus year with fewer than — analysts behind it gets a note. A year whose growth exceeds its return gets a note, and pays out less than nothing. A value that moves by — or more across the runways or returns gets a warning. A runway of — with growth of — or more gets a note.

Mature Stable

In Mature Stable, the market prices the stability of earnings [Damodaran 2024]. The route starts from today's earnings and grows them slowly.

  1. Normalized earnings (trailing earnings with one-off items removed) are the starting point.
  2. When the trailing margin stands far above its history, earnings are scaled down to the ten-year median margin.
  3. Earnings grow at terminal growth, —, in every year.
  4. No year grows above terminal growth, so the reinvestment charge starts at the runway return and fades in equal steps from year 1.
  5. It reaches the terminal return at the end of the runway, and uses it after.
  6. The cash, discounted at the hurdle rate and divided by the shares, is the value a share.

EPV (earnings power value: earnings divided by the hurdle rate, the value with no growth) stands beside the value. A company whose new capital earns more than the hurdle rate is worth more than its EPV.

The drawer also shows what the Mature Growth route would give, as a cross-check. The cross-check never sets the levels or the word.

High Growth and Young Growth

In High Growth, the market prices revenue growth and operating margins. In Young Growth, it prices revenue growth and gross margins [Damodaran 2024]. Earnings here are small or negative, so the route forecasts revenue and margins instead [Damodaran templates].

The revenue path

  1. Year 1 is the consensus revenue for the next twelve months.
  2. Years 2 and 3 grow at the consensus growth for the next two fiscal years.
  3. The year-3 rate, the held rate, continues until the high-growth period of — ends.
  4. The held rate is capped at the 90th percentile of five-year sales growth for companies of similar size [Base Rate Book].
  5. Growth then fades in equal steps over — to terminal growth.

The margin and the peers

The operating margin moves in a straight line from today's to the target margin (the margin a mature company of the same kind earns). It reaches the target after — and keeps it. A loss larger than revenue is a cost base, not a margin. For such a loss, operating income moves in dollars instead. It runs in a straight line from today's loss to the target margin's share of revenue by the same year. The Start-up route's ramp works the same way. The target is the median ten-year margin of the mature peers (the company's peers that the classifier puts in a mature phase). The peers come from the data vendor's peer list. At least — peers need — of margins each. With too few, the route takes the largest — mature companies of the company's own industry.

Reinvestment, tax and cash

Sales-to-capital (the revenue a dollar of new capital buys) sets what growth costs. Each year, the company invests next year's extra revenue divided by sales-to-capital. Fairband uses the company's own five-year measure when it is reliable, else the peers' median. An own measure below half the mature peers' median does not count as reliable. Operating profit is taxed at the country's statutory rate, after any losses earlier in the forecast [OECD]. Free cash flow is the profit after tax less the reinvestment.

After the last forecast year, a terminal value grows at terminal growth, charged at the terminal return. The terminal return is the hurdle rate. A company with a Wide moat may earn more on new capital, up to —. The cash and the terminal value, discounted at the hurdle rate, give the firm value. Cash is added, debt is taken off, and the result is divided by the shares.

What the price needs

The margin the price needs is the target margin at which the value equals today's price. Set it beside the peers' margins. A price that needs a margin above every peer's assumes the company will beat all of them. Fairband looks for it between — and —. The drawer also shows the growth the price needs in the held years.

Flags on this route

A held rate cut by the cap gets a note. Peers taken from the industry get a note. A loss larger than revenue, moved in dollars, gets a note. A return on new capital above the peers' median return gets a note. A value that moves by — or more across the peers' margins gets a warning.

No survival table fits a listed company worth more than —, so Fairband leaves that chance out. The value is then the going-concern value (the value if the company survives), and the drawer says so.

Decline

In Decline, the market prices book value [Damodaran 2024]. The route values a run-off (a business that shrinks year by year and frees capital as it shrinks) [Damodaran 2009a].

The run-off

  1. Years 1 to 3 follow the consensus revenue, as on the growth route.
  2. The consensus revenue continues past year 3, one year at a time, while at least — analysts give it and it stays above zero. After the consensus, revenue shrinks at the decline rate: the five-year revenue trend, as a share of last year's revenue. The rate fades to zero in a straight line by the last of the run-off's —. The decline rate never takes away more than half the revenue in a year.
  3. One operating margin holds in every year: the median of the last five fiscal margins.
  4. Each fall in revenue frees the capital that revenue needed. A sale recovers — of it.
  5. After —, a terminal value holds revenue flat: the business neither grows nor shrinks.
  6. The cash, discounted at the hurdle rate, plus cash on hand less debt, is the going-concern value.

The floor and distress

The liquidation floor is the net current asset value (current assets less all liabilities) a share [Graham]. When it is above zero and above the going-concern value, it replaces that value. Interest cover is operating profit divided by interest expense. It gives a synthetic rating (the bond rating such cover usually earns) [Damodaran ratings]. Each rating carries a ten-year chance of default [Damodaran 2009a]. In distress, a forced sale recovers — of the firm value. The floored value is the going-concern value or the net current asset value, whichever the floor gives. The value weights the floored value and the forced-sale value by that chance.

What the price needs

The implied decline rate is the decline rate at which the value equals today's price. The consensus years stay as they are, and the rate fades to zero as above. Set the implied decline rate beside the decline rate the trend gives. A price that needs revenue to shrink more slowly than it has assumes a turnaround.

Three distress signals

Three signals warn and never change the value.

  • Altman's Z″ is a score of bankruptcy risk, built from four ratios from the balance sheet and the income statement [Altman 2000]. Below — it is in the distress zone, and above — in the safe zone.
  • Piotroski's F (nine tests of financial health, one point each) at — or below is in the weakest group [Piotroski 2000].
  • Capital spending above depreciation while revenue shrinks is reinvesting in decline.

Start-up

In a Start-up, the market prices the potential market and access to capital [Damodaran 2024]. Fairband gives a Start-up no value. The inputs that would make one, the market's size and the company's share of it, are judgments. The engine holds no judgments, so the row is always a refusal that keeps its figures.

What the price needs

Instead, the route states the revenue in year ten that makes the company worth its price today [Damodaran 2009b]. Revenue rises in a straight line from today's to that figure. Operating profit rises in a straight line to the mature peers' median margin on it. The peers, tax, reinvestment and the terminal value work as on the growth route. The price here is the market value plus debt, less cash.

The flags

A flag warns and never changes a value, a level or the word. Each has a short word for the screen and one sentence in the drawer. Eleven flags are quality warnings (signs that the value may be wrong because the business or its figures are unusual). The Flags column counts them, and the Clean filter keeps the rows with none. Every other flag is a note: shown, never counted.

Quality warnings

  • Large non-recurring item large-nri Reported earnings differ from earnings without one-off items by — or more.
  • Value spread value-spread The value moves by — or more across the runways, returns or peer margins tested.
  • Margin above history margin-flag The trailing margin is — its ten-year median or more. It also fires on a margin above the ten-year high by — of the high. It needs — of margins. Its word reads Newly profitable or Possible cycle peak where that fits better.
  • Z″ distress z-distress A Decline company's Altman Z″ is below —.
  • Low F-score f-score-low A Decline company's Piotroski F is — or lower.
  • Reinvesting in decline reinvesting-in-decline A Decline company spends more on capital than its depreciation while revenue shrinks.
  • Equity raise equity-raise Shares issued over the last year come to — of the market value or more.
  • Negative free cash flow negative-fcf Operating cash flow less capital spending was below zero over the last year.
  • Heavy capex heavy-capex Capital spending over the last year was above — of operating cash flow.
  • Cash cover short burn-cover-short A Start-up's cash covers less than — of its burn.
  • Consensus recovery consensus-recovery A Decline company's consensus has revenue growing while the run-off shrinks it.

Notes

Notes say how a figure was reached. They show in the Flags column and the drawer, and never count.

  • Growth word disagrees growth-word-disagrees The growth word points to another phase than the margin rule gave.
  • Loss year loss-year An established company had a loss year and was placed as if it were profitable.
  • Revenue gap revenue-gap An established company had no revenue three years ago, so it is not a Start-up.
  • Revenue falling revenue-falling Revenue fell over five years, but in too few years to make a Decline.
  • Margin direction disagrees margin-direction-disagrees The margin's direction points to another phase.
  • Free cash flow disagrees fcf-sign Free cash flow has the unusual sign for the phase.
  • Short history short-history Fewer than five fiscal years are on file, or growth spans fewer than three.
  • Zero margin margin-zero The deciding margin is exactly zero, and counts as positive.
  • Currency change currency-change The reporting currency changed between fiscal years.
  • Currency unknown currency-unknown No statement gives the reporting currency, so the company is treated as a foreign filer.
  • Tax rate carried forward tax-carried-forward A fiscal year newer than the tax table takes the table's newest rate.
  • Leases not on balance sheet leases-not-on-balance-sheet A balance sheet from before the current lease rules shows no lease assets.
  • Trailing on fiscal year trailing-on-fiscal-year The last four quarters on file do not follow each other, so the last fiscal year stands in.
  • SBC basis unknown sbc-basis-unknown Fairband could not tell whether the consensus counts stock compensation as a cost.
  • Path on EPS path-on-eps Years 2 and 3 grow at the consensus earnings a share, not net income.
  • Few analysts few-analysts A consensus year has fewer than — analysts behind it.
  • Step series disagree step-series-disagree Year 2 or 3 grows at rates more than 5 points apart on the consensus net income and earnings a share.
  • Path held path-held A consensus year is missing, so it holds the rate of the year before.
  • Path on trend path-on-trend A Decline company without a usable consensus shrinks at the decline rate in years 1 to 3, before the fade.
  • Decline rate floored decline-rate-floored The trend takes away more than half the revenue a year, so the run-off uses that floor.
  • Above base rate consensus-above-base-rate The year-3 consensus growth is above the cap, so the held years take the cap.
  • Industry peers peers-from-industry Too few listed peers were mature, so the peers come from the company's industry.
  • ROIC unavailable roic-unavailable Fewer than five years of return on capital are on file.
  • ROIC on fewer years roic-on-n-years Five to nine years of return on capital are on file, not ten.
  • Ten-year ROIC unavailable ten-year-roic-unavailable The ten years' capital sums to zero or less.
  • Line not presented line-not-presented:… A balance-sheet line is missing and counts as nil.
  • Charge zeroed charge-zeroed Measured capital is zero or less, so the runway years carry no charge.
  • Return floored return-floored The company's return on new capital was below the hurdle rate, so the hurdle rate replaced it.
  • Growth above return growth-above-return The revenue growth a year pays for exceeds its return, so that year pays out less than nothing.
  • Tax losses unknown tax-loss-history-unknown The forecast has a loss year, and past tax losses are not on file.
  • Ramp in dollars margin-ramp-in-dollars The trailing loss is larger than revenue, so operating income moves to the target margin in dollars.
  • Return above peers return-above-peers The return on new capital at the target margin is above the peers' median return.
  • Failure not charged failure-not-charged A Young Growth value assumes the company survives.
  • NCAV floor ncav-floor The net current asset value is above the run-off's value, and sets the floor.
  • Distress not charged distress-not-charged No interest expense or no debt line is on file, so no chance of default applies.
  • Below net cash below-net-cash A Start-up's market value is at or below its net cash.
  • Consensus basis unknown consensus-basis-unknown A mature company has a large non-recurring item, and the consensus does not say whether its forecasts count such items.
  • Long runway, high growth long-runway-high-growth A runway of — carries a belief or implied growth of — or more.

Agents and reviews

Overrides and the reviewed value

An override is a change an agent (a model that researches one company through Fairband's tools) makes to one input of the engine. It carries a reason and evidence, such as a filing or a finding (a dated fact with its source). The engine values the company again under its overrides: the reviewed value. An agent never writes a value. The mechanical value (the engine's value with no override) does not move.

An agent may change sixteen inputs, such as the phase, the belief and the moat, each within set bounds. Locked inputs, such as the hurdle rate, terminal growth and the reported figures, take no override. An agent adds and drops overrides; you read them. A refresh also drops a period-tied override (one that corrects a single period's figure) once newer statements arrive or that figure changes. The review (the overrides and the value they give) moves to each new snapshot, the company's figures from a refresh. An overridden input is tagged JUDGMENT, beside DATA (a figure from the vendor) and RULE (a value from the policy).

The bridge

The bridge leads from the mechanical value to the reviewed value, one line for each override. A line names the input, its old and new figure, and the change in the value a share. The changes add up to the difference.

Mid-cycle earnings

A cyclical business (one whose profits rise and fall with its industry's cycle, such as memory chips or steel) earns most at a peak. The engine does not judge whether a business is cyclical. Mature Stable scales earnings down to the ten-year median margin when the margin stands far above its history. The margin flag may then read Possible cycle peak. Otherwise a peak's earnings stand. An agent who finds a business cyclical bases the reviewed value on mid-cycle earnings (what it earns across a whole cycle). On the Mature Growth and Mature Stable routes, an override takes the starting earnings to mid-cycle, after any such scaling.

Guidance

A guidance record is a figure the company itself gave on an earnings call for a future period. Only a clear full-year figure can reach the reviewed value: a number or a range for a stated metric, on the reported basis. A range takes the midpoint. It may replace year 1 (the next twelve months' consensus) or the consensus growth of year 2 or 3 (a consensus step). It does so only through an override that names the record. A refresh drops an override of year 1 once its guided year has passed. Using guidance is the agent's best effort, never required. The mechanical value never reads guidance. Other guidance is discussed in the report; the consensus stands.

The secondary valuation

Where neither the route nor the review values the company, the report carries a secondary valuation: the fallback. It is a value a share by a standard discounted cash flow [Damodaran templates]. Where the route or the review values the company there is none: the engine refuses the call, so no second value weighs on the stance. The engine projects the cash flows from drivers the agent sets: revenue growth and margin by year, a tax rate, sales-to-capital and a terminal growth. Or the agent gives the cash flows itself, one part or a sum of parts (a company valued segment by segment). The engine discounts them at the locked hurdle rate, —. The terminal growth is the agent's choice, at most the locked terminal growth, —.

A grid shows the value a share at the hurdle rate and one point either side, across three terminal growths. It shows how much of the value is assumption, and is never a value. A foreign filer, or a row with no diluted share count, can have no secondary valuation; its report says why.

The sensitivity reading

A sensitivity reading is a grid of the route's value across up to five values of each of two inputs. A reading overrides nothing: the reviewed value does not move.

The stance

The stance is the agent's word: buy, hold, trim or avoid. The engine sets none, and the designation stays a fact about the price. The agent weighs the price against the reviewed value's levels; on a refused row with none, the secondary valuation's. The report's thesis argues the stance.

The report

A full report, the agent's written research on one company, has twelve sections, the last earnings call among them. A valuation review has ten, without “The business” and “Industry and competition”. Every number in a report cites a figure Fairband stores. A number from outside Fairband enters as a finding or a guidance record. The server checks every cite before it publishes, and rejects a report with any failure whole. The report page marks each cited number with a footnote; the references follow the last section.

Where to read them

The screen's Reviewed column shows the reviewed value. Where neither the route nor the review values the company, it shows the value of a secondary valuation instead. The band, the buy and trim levels and the designation follow the reviewed value; the Mechanical column keeps the engine's value. The Stance column shows the latest report's word. A ◆ after the ticker marks a published report. The drawer's meter draws that band; the drawer adds the stance and the report's summary. Its Details hold the bridge, the overrides, the secondary valuation, the guidance, the judgments (the agent's decisions on the inputs) and the notes. The Inbox lists the work items (requests for an agent's research on one company). Request research in the drawer posts one. On a watchlist, it posts a valuation review for each company on the list with no open item.
